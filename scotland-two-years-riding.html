Two Years on Two Wheels: Riding Scotland's Roads (2015–2017)

By Nandkishor Sharma · 12 June 2017
Share: 💬 WhatsApp 𝕏 Post Copied!

Long before I ever threw a leg over a motorcycle in the United Arab Emirates, and years before carving the high-altitude sweepers of Jebel Jais or blasting across the sun-drenched desert tarmac of Emirates Road, my riding soul was forged in the wet, windswept wilderness of Scotland. Between 2015 and 2017, I spent two transformative years exploring the Scottish Highlands, the lowlands of Fife, and the Atlantic coastal fringes on two wheels.

To an outside observer, no two motorcycling environments could be further apart. The UAE is defined by endless sunshine, bone-dry desert highways, four-lane asphalt, and ambient temperatures that test the upper limits of human heat endurance. Scotland, by contrast, is a land of moody grey skies, ancient glacial valleys, moss-covered drystone walls, single-track mountain passes, and rain that blows sideways off the North Atlantic. Yet paradoxically, it was the relentless challenges of Scotland that built the foundational roadcraft, mechanical discipline, and mental resilience that keep me safe on a superbike today.

1. The Four-Seasons-in-an-Hour Reality

In Scotland, you do not check the weather forecast to decide whether to ride; if you waited for guaranteed sunshine, your motorcycle would spend eleven months of the year under a dust cover. Instead, you learn to embrace the old Highland proverb: "There is no such thing as bad weather, only unsuitable clothing."

A typical Saturday morning ride departing Edinburgh or Glasgow might begin under crisp, pale blue skies with temperatures hovering around 10°C. Within an hour, as you skirt the wooded shores of Loch Lomond on the A82, a thick coastal sea mist (known locally as haar) rolls in, dropping visibility to thirty metres and coating your helmet shield in micro-droplets. Climbing past the Bridge of Orchy onto the vast, barren expanse of Rannoch Moor, the heavens open into a torrential Atlantic downpour. And thirty minutes later, descending the dramatic glacial amphitheatre of Glencoe, the clouds part to reveal dazzling sunlight glinting off dark volcanic rockfaces.

Riding through these rapid atmospheric swings teaches you an invaluable lesson: you cannot rely on static assumptions. Every mile of road presents a shifting friction coefficient. You learn to read the subtle color changes in the bitumen — spotting where dark, saturated tarmac transitions into treacherous standing puddles or where a sudden temperature drop has created invisible black ice across a shaded mountain bridge.

2. Surviving the Highland Chill: The Layering Architecture

Cold weather riding is not merely uncomfortable; it is physiologically dangerous. Wind chill is governed by aggressive physics: cruising at 100 km/h in 5°C ambient air subjects your body to an effective wind chill temperature of roughly -3°C. Within forty minutes, peripheral blood vessels constrict to protect core vital organs, draining warm blood from your hands and feet. Stiff, numb fingers lose fine motor tactile sensitivity, delaying front brake reaction times by several critical tenths of a second.

  • The Tri-Layer System: One bulky winter jacket fails because it restricts upper body movement and traps sweat. The solution is active layering: an ultra-fine Merino wool base layer against the skin (which retains thermal insulation even when damp), a lightweight fleece or wind-blocking softshell mid-layer to trap air, and a 100% waterproof laminated Gore-Tex outer shell that prevents external wind penetration.
  • Heated Ergonomics: In Scotland, high-output heated handlebar grips (such as Oxford HotGrips) and heated inner glove liners transition from luxury accessories into vital safety equipment, keeping wrist flexor tendons warm and responsive.
  • The Visor Struggle: In cold, damp mountain air, your warm breath causes standard helmet visors to fog up instantly. My two years in Scotland drilled home the absolute necessity of a sealed Pinlock anti-fog lens insert, breath deflectors, and chin-curtain wind blockers.

3. Single-Track Roadcraft and the Highland Highway Code

Once you venture north of Fort William into the remote Northwest Highlands and onto the route that has now become world-famous as the North Coast 500 (NC500), multi-lane roads vanish. You find yourself navigating hundreds of miles of single-track tarmac barely wide enough for a single vehicle.

These roads are punctuated every few hundred metres by diamond-shaped white signs marking Passing Places. Riding single tracks demands a high-bandwidth brand of road awareness completely alien to city riders:

  • Reading the Distant Horizon: You do not look at the road immediately ahead. You scan the hillside two kilometres in the distance, spotting the rooflines of oncoming campervans or logging trucks and calculating which passing bay between you provides the optimal deceleration rendezvous.
  • Blind Crest Deceleration: Highland roads follow natural glacial contours, featuring sharp roller-coaster crests where the tarmac drops away blindly. Cresting a blind brow at 80 km/h is suicidal; a herd of Scottish Blackface sheep or a stationary tourist car is frequently waiting on the other side. You learn to check your speed and hug your side of the crown over every single rise.
  • Highland Livestock Awareness: Sheep in the Highlands have zero road sense. Ewes with young lambs often stand on opposite sides of the tarmac. The moment an exhaust note approaches, the lamb will panic and dart across the road directly into your path. Spotting livestock early and rolling off the throttle to idle past is mandatory.
In Scotland, you learn that riding within your sightline is the ultimate law. If you cannot stop completely within the distance of road you can see to be clear, you are gambling with your life.

4. Legendary Corridors: From Glencoe to Bealach na Bà

While the riding conditions could be brutal, the rewards were breathtaking. Scotland possesses some of the most magnificent mountain passes on earth:

  • The A82 Through Glencoe: Riding beneath the towering pyramid of Buachaille Etive Mòr and carving between the Three Sisters peaks is a deeply spiritual experience. The road sweeps gracefully through wide, cambered turns bordered by ancient peat bogs and tumbling waterfalls.
  • The Pass of the Cattle (Bealach na Bà): Rising from the sea-level shores of Loch Kishorn over the mountains of the Applecross peninsula, this is the historic drover's road. Climbing to 2,053 feet (626 metres), it features the steepest ascent of any road in the UK with gradients reaching 20%. The final summit approach consists of a dizzying sequence of tight, single-track Alpine switchback hairpins perched on sheer cliff edges. Squeezing a motorcycle through those hairpins in thick mountain mist requires absolute clutch finesse and resolute vision control.
  • Loch Lomond Sweepers: The winding shoreline of the A82 north of Tarbet provides an incredible training ground for mid-corner vision and smooth throttle roll-on, with the dark loch waters lapping just feet from the crash barriers.

5. The Scottish Legacy in the Desert

When I relocated to the United Arab Emirates and purchased a motorcycle in Dubai, friends remarked on how vastly different my riding life was going to be. And on the surface, they were right: I had traded Gore-Tex waterproof suits for perforated mesh, heated grips for CamelBak hydration bladders, and Highland mist for the golden glow of the Rub' al Khali desert.

Yet beneath the surface, the core lessons of Scotland govern every single kilometer I ride in the Middle East. Scotland taught me profound mechanical empathy — understanding how cold tyres grip differently than warm ones, how moisture degrades brake bite, and how road surfaces can conceal danger beneath an innocent sheen. Most of all, Scotland instilled a permanent, deep respect for nature. Whether you are facing a sudden Atlantic storm on Rannoch Moor or a 46°C desert crosswind on the road to Fujairah, the rule remains identical: check your ego at the garage door, respect the machine, and ride with margin to spare.

⚠️ Rider Safety & UAE Road Protocol

Public roads across Dubai and the UAE are not racetracks. Always observe local speed regulations, hold your lane through mountain passes, wear certified safety gear (ECE 22.06 / CE Level 2), and stay hydrated during warm riding hours. Ride within your limits.
