The Pre-Ride Heat Checklist: Getting Your Superbike Ready for Dubai Summers

By Nandkishor Sharma · 04 August 2026 · 6 min read

Riding a superbike through a Dubai summer is a different sport from riding one in October. The bike doesn't care about the calendar, but heat changes tyre pressure, fluid behaviour, rider stamina and reaction time — all of it, before you've even left the driveway. This is the checklist I actually run through, not the generic one.

1. Tyres: check them cold, not after they've sat in the sun

Ambient asphalt temperature in peak Dubai summer can sit well above 60°C. Tyre pressure climbs fast once you're rolling, so setting pressure after the bike has been parked in direct sun already gives you a false baseline.

  • Check pressure early morning, before the bike has been in the sun
  • Stay within manufacturer spec — don't underinflate to "compensate" for heat, it overheats the carcass faster
  • Inspect tread depth and look for any sidewall bulging, which shows up faster in extreme heat

2. Fluids: coolant and brake fluid do the real work

Your engine and brakes are fighting ambient heat before they've done any work at all.

  • Confirm coolant level and check for any discoloration signaling it's overdue
  • Brake fluid should be changed on schedule — heat accelerates moisture absorption, which lowers the boiling point right when you need it most
  • Chain lube in extreme heat burns off faster; lube more frequently than the manual suggests in summer months
The best gear for Dubai heat isn't less gear — it's gear that manages heat instead of trapping it.

3. Gear: mesh, not minimal

The instinct in extreme heat is to wear less. Resist it. Perforated mesh jackets and pants with CE armor breathe far better than a t-shirt while still doing their job in a fall. Look for:

  • Full mesh riding jacket and pants, not just a mesh panel
  • Ventilated, full-face helmet with a functioning chin vent
  • Gloves with perforation across the palm and back of hand

4. Timing: ride the temperature, not the clock

The single biggest lever you control is when you ride.

  • Early morning (before 7am) or after sunset are the only windows worth planning long rides in during peak summer
  • Avoid midday rides beyond short, necessary trips — heat exhaustion symptoms can appear before you consciously feel "hot"
  • Build in shaded stops every 30–40 minutes on longer rides, even if you don't feel like you need one yet

5. You are part of the machine

Dehydration affects reaction time before it affects how you feel. Start hydrating well before the ride, not once you're already thirsty, and carry water on anything longer than a short loop. A hydration pack under mesh gear is worth the small extra weight.

Quick reference: before you thumb the starter

  • Tyre pressure checked cold, within spec
  • Coolant and brake fluid levels confirmed
  • Chain lubed if it's been more than a few rides
  • Full mesh gear, ventilated helmet, perforated gloves
  • Ride window is early morning or post-sunset
  • Water carried, hydration started an hour before

None of this is complicated. It's just easy to skip when you're eager to ride — and it's exactly the stuff that turns a good ride into a rough one, or a rough one into a dangerous one, in a Gulf summer.

Best Early-Morning Ride Routes Out of Dubai

By Nandkishor Sharma · 15 April 2026 · 5 min read

The best riding in Dubai happens before most of the city wakes up. Empty roads, cooler air, and long, clean stretches of tarmac — here are four routes worth setting an alarm for, all doable as a there-and-back before the heat sets in.

1. Hatta Road (E44 stretch)

The classic. Wide, well-surfaced sweepers with the Hajar Mountains rising on both sides as you get further out. Leave by 5:30am and you'll have most of the road to yourself.

  • Distance: roughly 100km one-way from central Dubai to Hatta
  • Fuel up before leaving — stations thin out past the city limits
  • Food stop: grab breakfast at one of the small cafés in Hatta town before heading back

📍 Open Hatta route in Google Maps →

2. Al Qudra Road

Flatter and more technical than Hatta — long straights broken by tighter corners near the cycle track section. Popular with cyclists from 6am onward, so throttle discipline matters here more than top speed.

  • Distance: the full loop runs about 85km
  • Watch for cyclists and photographers near the lakes section
  • Food stop: the food trucks near Al Qudra Lakes open early and are worth the stop

📍 Open Al Qudra route in Google Maps →

3. Jebel Jais Mountain Road

Technically in Ras Al Khaimah, but close enough for a Dubai-based early ride. The UAE's highest road, with proper elevation change, hairpins, and noticeably cooler air near the top.

  • Distance: around 130km one-way from Dubai to the summit road
  • Start earlier than the other routes — the extra distance and climb take longer
  • Food stop: cafés near the base in Jais village, or push on to the viewing deck first

📍 Open Jebel Jais route in Google Maps →

Empty roads and cool air don't last past 8am in summer — that window is the whole point.

4. Dubai–Al Ain Road

Straighter and faster than the mountain routes, good for a longer, more relaxed early ride if you want distance over technical corners.

  • Distance: about 130km one-way
  • Well-lit and well-serviced — better option if you're still building night/dawn riding confidence
  • Food stop: Al Ain has plenty of options once you arrive, worth a proper breakfast stop before the ride back

📍 Open Al Ain route in Google Maps →

Before you head out

  • Leave before 6am in summer months — heat builds fast after sunrise
  • Tell someone your route, especially on the mountain roads
  • Carry water even on a "short" 100km run — mountain routes have long stretches with no stops

None of these need to be ridden hard to be worth it. Half the appeal is just being out before the city — and everyone else — wakes up.

Chain, Sprocket & Brake Wear: A Simple Log Sheet

By Nandkishor Sharma · 18 January 2025 · 5 min read

You don't need a workshop background to catch chain, sprocket and brake wear before it becomes a problem — you just need to actually look, on a schedule, and write down what you see. Here's the simple log I keep, and what each check is actually telling you.

⬇ Download the blank log template (CSV) →

1. Chain: check every 500–800km

Chain wear compounds — a slightly stretched chain wears the sprockets faster, which wears the new chain faster once you replace it. Catching it early saves you replacing all three at once.

  • Pull the chain away from the rear sprocket at its midpoint — more than a sliver of daylight between chain and sprocket teeth means it's stretching
  • Check for tight spots by rotating the wheel — the chain shouldn't feel noticeably tighter at any one point
  • Log slack measurement at the tightest point each time, so you can see the trend, not just a single reading

2. Sprockets: check whenever you check the chain

Sprocket teeth wear into a hooked or shark-fin shape as they age — this is the clearest visual sign, easier to spot than measuring the chain itself.

  • Look at the teeth side-on — worn teeth lean instead of standing straight
  • Compare front and rear sprocket wear — they rarely wear at exactly the same rate
  • Log a simple visual rating each check: good / early wear / replace soon

3. Brake pads: check every 1,000km or monthly

Pad material wears faster than most riders expect, especially with mixed city and highway riding.

  • Check remaining pad thickness through the caliper — most pads have a wear line stamped into the material
  • Listen for a metallic scraping sound at low speed — a sign the pad has worn down to the backing plate
  • Log the thinnest pad's thickness in mm, not just "looks fine" — numbers catch trends words don't
A number on a log sheet catches a trend three checks before your eyes do.

4. Brake fluid: check monthly, replace on schedule

  • Fluid level should sit between the min/max lines on the reservoir
  • Colour matters — fresh fluid is close to clear or light amber; dark, cloudy fluid means it's overdue
  • Log the last full fluid change date so you're not relying on memory for the service interval

A simple log format that works

You don't need software for this — a notes app entry or a page in a notebook is enough. Each entry just needs:

  • Date and odometer reading
  • Chain slack measurement (mm)
  • Sprocket visual rating (good / early wear / replace soon)
  • Thinnest brake pad reading (mm)
  • Brake fluid colour and level (OK / due for change)

Five minutes with the bike on its stand, once every week or two, catches almost everything before it becomes an expensive or dangerous surprise.

Riding a Superbike in the Rain: What Actually Changes

By Nandkishor Sharma · 18 January 2025 · 5 min read

UAE rain is rare enough that most riders never build real habits around it — which is exactly why the first heavy rain of the season sees a spike in bike accidents every year. The roads aren't built for sustained rain, and neither is most riders' muscle memory. Here's what actually changes when it's wet.

1. The first 20 minutes of rain are the most dangerous

Rain lifts oil and rubber residue that's been baking into the tarmac for months, turning the road surface into something closer to a skating rink until it washes clean.

  • If you're already riding when rain starts, slow down immediately and increase following distance
  • Avoid painted road markings and metal surfaces (manhole covers, expansion joints) — they get slippery fastest
  • If possible, pull over and wait 15-20 minutes rather than riding through the worst of it

2. Braking distance changes more than you expect

  • Brake earlier and more progressively — grab a wet brake the way you'd grab a dry one and you'll lock a wheel
  • Use both brakes together, smoothly, rather than relying heavily on the front
  • Leave significantly more following distance than feels necessary — your instinct will underestimate it
Standing water hides the road's actual condition — treat every puddle as unknown depth until proven otherwise.

3. Visibility works both ways

  • Turn your headlight on even in daylight rain — being seen matters as much as seeing
  • Avoid riding directly behind large vehicles — spray from trucks and buses can blind you for seconds at a time
  • A clear or anti-fog visor matters more than looks in genuine rain — fogging is a real hazard on UAE humidity

4. Gear that actually matters in rain

  • Waterproof or waterproof-over gear beats "resistant" gear — resistant gear fails after 20-30 minutes of sustained rain
  • Waterproof gloves specifically — cold, wet hands lose grip feel fast, and that's exactly when you need it most
  • Boots that seal at the ankle, not just water-resistant uppers

5. Standing water and flooded underpasses

UAE's flat terrain means water pools fast in underpasses and low-lying roads during heavy rain.

  • If you can't see the road surface through the water, don't ride through it — depth and debris are both invisible
  • Take the longer route around a flooded underpass rather than risk it
  • If already committed, keep steady low-to-mid throttle rather than braking or accelerating hard mid-water

Quick reference for wet rides

  • Headlight on, even in daylight
  • Following distance at least doubled
  • Brake earlier, more gently, using both brakes
  • Avoid painted lines, metal surfaces, standing water
  • Full waterproof gear, not just "resistant"

Rain riding isn't about riding scared — it's about riding with a wider margin. The bike behaves differently for a reason; ride like it.

Race Day at Knockhill: My First Track Day in Scotland

By Nandkishor Sharma · 20 September 2016 · 5 min read

Knockhill Racing Circuit sits in Fife, Scotland — a short, technical track that punishes anyone who thinks track riding is just "going faster." My first track day there taught me more in one afternoon than months of road riding had.

1. The track humbles you immediately

Road confidence means nothing on a circuit. Riders half my road experience were carrying more corner speed through Duffus Dip and the Chicane than I was, simply because they'd learned proper racing lines and I hadn't.

  • A track day instructor walking the circuit with you before your first session is worth more than any amount of solo practice
  • Braking markers exist for a reason — use the ones the circuit provides rather than guessing
  • Your road riding lean angle and a track's actual usable lean angle are two different things — sighting laps matter

2. Tyres and pressure change everything

  • Cold Scottish track mornings mean tyres need real warm-up laps before pushing — no shortcuts
  • Track-day pressure is not road pressure — check what the circuit and your tyre manufacturer recommend, not your usual number
  • Watch tyre wear indicators between sessions — a full day of track riding chews through rubber faster than weeks of road riding
Knockhill's elevation changes mean the track is honest — it shows you exactly where your technique breaks down.

3. What actually improved my riding

  • Trail braking into Clark Corner taught me more about front-end feel than years of road riding had
  • Watching faster riders' lines through the Chicane and copying their reference points, not their speed
  • Learning to look through a corner rather than at the apron — the bike genuinely follows your eyes

If you're doing your first track day

  • Book the beginner/novice group, not the intermediate one, regardless of your road riding confidence
  • Bring more tyre pressure gauge checks than you think you'll need
  • Focus on smoothness over speed for the first two sessions — speed follows technique, not the other way round

I left Knockhill that day slower than half the grid and happier than I'd been on a bike in years. That's what a good track day actually gives you — not lap times, but a real look at how much there still is to learn.

Two Years on Two Wheels: Riding Scotland's Roads (2015–2017)

By Nandkishor Sharma · 12 June 2017 · 6 min read

Before Dubai's heat and highways, there were two years of riding Scotland's roads — a completely different kind of challenge. Where UAE riding tests you against heat, Scotland tests you against everything else: cold, rain, single-track roads, and sheep that do not care about your right of way.

1. The roads themselves are the reward

Scotland's driving/riding roads don't need a destination to justify the ride — the A82 along Loch Lomond, the sweeping runs through Glencoe, and sections of what's now called the North Coast 500 are worth riding purely for the road itself.

  • Single-track roads with passing places demand a different kind of awareness than anything in city riding — blind crests hide oncoming traffic constantly
  • Livestock on the road is a real, frequent hazard in the Highlands, not a rare warning sign — sheep move unpredictably and often in groups
  • Weather can change within a single ride — what starts dry at the coast can turn to rain and fog by the time you reach higher ground

2. Cold and wet riding is a skill you have to build

  • Layering matters more than any single piece of "warm" gear — a proper base layer under waterproofs beats one thick jacket
  • Heated grips stopped being a luxury and became close to essential for anything beyond an hour in Scottish winter conditions
  • Visor fogging in cold, damp air is a constant fight — a pinlock insert made a bigger difference than any anti-fog spray
Scotland doesn't test your speed — it tests your patience, your layering, and your ability to read weather that changes by the mile.

3. What two years of Scottish riding actually taught me

  • Reading a road's surface became second nature — wet cattle grids, mossy patches, and loose gravel on single tracks all needed constant attention
  • Riding within visibility rather than within speed limit — Highland fog can drop visibility to almost nothing within minutes
  • Real patience with slower traffic on single-track roads — there's often nowhere safe to overtake until the next passing place

4. Favourite stretches worth knowing

  • A82 along Loch Lomond — sweeping curves with the loch running alongside for most of the ride
  • Glencoe — dramatic elevation and some of the most photographed riding roads in Scotland, best ridden early to avoid tourist traffic
  • Applecross Pass (Bealach na Ba) — steep hairpins and one of the highest road climbs in the UK, not for a first ride in bad weather

Moving to UAE riding after two years in Scotland felt like learning a new sport — heat instead of cold, wide highways instead of single tracks. But the fundamentals Scotland taught me about reading conditions and riding within what you can actually see have never stopped being useful, wherever the ride happens to be.

Superbike Insurance in the UAE: How It Actually Works

By Nandkishor Sharma · 18 August 2026 · 5 min read

Most riders buy the cheapest insurance policy they can find, then find out what it actually covers the day they need it. Superbike insurance in the UAE has a few quirks that are worth understanding before that day, not after.

1. Third-party vs comprehensive

UAE law requires at least third-party insurance to register any vehicle, including motorcycles. But for a superbike, third-party-only is a real gamble.

  • Third-party covers damage you cause to others — not your own bike, at all
  • Comprehensive covers your own bike too, including theft and accident damage
  • Given superbike repair and parts costs in the UAE, comprehensive is worth the premium difference for most riders

2. Why superbikes cost more to insure

Insurers price motorcycles by engine capacity, bike value, and rider age/experience — superbikes hit all three factors at once.

  • Higher cc engines are statistically linked to higher claim frequency and severity
  • Parts and panels for sport bikes are expensive to source and replace
  • Younger or newly-licensed riders usually pay a noticeably higher premium
Read the exclusions list before the premium — it tells you more about what you're actually buying.

3. Common exclusions to check for

This is the part riders skip, and the part that matters most when a claim gets filed.

  • Racing, track days, and competitive riding are excluded from almost every standard policy — you need a separate track-day policy or rider
  • Riding without a valid UAE motorcycle license class voids most claims
  • Modifications (exhaust, ECU tuning, aftermarket parts) can affect a claim if not declared to the insurer
  • Some policies exclude single-vehicle accidents (you crashing alone, no other party involved) — confirm this explicitly

4. What actually affects your premium

  • No-claims history — riding claim-free for a year or more meaningfully lowers renewal premiums
  • Where you park and store the bike overnight (covered parking vs street parking can matter for theft cover)
  • Agreed value vs market value cover — agreed value avoids disputes over payout at claim time, especially useful on a bike that's been well maintained

Before you sign anything

  • Confirm comprehensive cover, not just third-party
  • Ask directly: is track riding excluded?
  • Declare every modification up front, in writing
  • Check whether cover is agreed value or market value
  • Confirm your license class matches what the bike requires

None of this is exciting reading, but it's the difference between an insurance policy that actually pays out when you need it, and one that just looks good on paper.

Riding Modes and Traction Control: What They Actually Do

By Nandkishor Sharma · 25 August 2026 · 5 min read

Most riders leave their bike in whatever mode it came in and never touch the traction control settings again. That's leaving real safety margin and real performance on the table — here's what these systems actually control, in plain terms.

1. Riding modes: it's about power delivery, not just top speed

Modes like Rain, Road, Sport, and Track don't just cap your top speed — they reshape how power arrives at the rear wheel.

  • Rain/Wet mode softens throttle response and caps peak power — the bike feels calmer even at the same throttle position
  • Road mode gives a more linear, predictable curve for everyday riding
  • Sport/Track modes sharpen throttle response and unlock full power, but demand more precise inputs from you

2. Traction control: what it's actually watching

Traction control compares front and rear wheel speed constantly — when the rear spins faster than the front (a sign of slipping), it intervenes.

  • Intervention isn't binary — most systems cut power gradually, not with a hard on/off cutout
  • Higher TC levels intervene earlier and more aggressively — better for wet roads or unfamiliar surfaces
  • Lower TC levels give more wheel slip before intervening — useful on track, riskier on the street
Traction control is a safety net, not a substitute for reading the road surface yourself.

3. When to actually change your settings

  • Switching to Rain mode the moment roads go wet isn't overcautious — it's exactly what the mode is built for
  • Dropping TC intervention low on the street, especially in UAE heat where tarmac can behave unpredictably, removes a safety margin most riders don't need to remove
  • New-to-a-bike riders benefit from spending real time in Road mode before exploring Sport — the power delivery difference is bigger than the spec sheet suggests

4. Common misunderstandings

  • Traction control doesn't prevent low-speed drops or crashes from braking mistakes — it only manages rear-wheel slip under power
  • A higher mode number isn't always "better" — Track mode with TC turned low is not the safer everyday choice
  • Modes reset to default on some bikes after a key cycle — worth checking so you're not accidentally riding in a mode you didn't choose

Quick reference

  • Wet or unfamiliar roads → Rain mode, higher TC intervention
  • Daily commuting → Road mode, mid TC
  • Confident, dry, familiar roads → Sport mode, but keep TC on
  • Track only → Track mode, lower TC, with proper training

These systems exist to extend your margin, not replace your judgment. Learn what each setting actually changes, and pick deliberately — not just whatever mode the bike happened to start in.

Buying Guide

Buying Your First Superbike: What Actually Matters

By Nandkishor Sharma · 30 August 2026 · 6 min read

Everyone's first question is which bike to buy. That's the wrong first question. The right ones are about power delivery, ergonomics, and honesty about where you actually ride — the bike comes after those answers, not before.

1. Start with how you'll actually ride, not how you imagine riding

Most new superbike owners ride 80% city and highway commuting, and 20% the kind of riding they bought the bike for.

  • If most of your riding will be city traffic, a more upright, forgiving ergonomic position beats an aggressive full-race crouch
  • Be honest about how often you'll actually get to a track or open mountain road — it's usually less than you plan
  • Weight matters more in city riding than raw horsepower — a lighter bike is easier to filter through traffic and manage at low speed

2. Power delivery matters more than peak horsepower

A bike's spec sheet horsepower number tells you almost nothing about how it actually feels to ride day to day.

  • Look for how power is delivered across the rev range, not just the peak number — a linear, predictable power curve is far easier to manage as a newer rider
  • Ride-by-wire bikes with selectable power modes give you a genuine "detuned" option while you build confidence
  • Traction control and ABS aren't training wheels — they're real safety margin, especially in the first year
The best first superbike is the one that lets you make small mistakes without them becoming big ones.

3. New vs used: the real trade-offs

  • A used bike from a private seller can save real money, but get a pre-purchase inspection from an independent mechanic — not the seller's guy
  • Check service history specifically for chain, valve clearance, and any crash damage repairs
  • New bikes come with warranty and dealer support, which matters more than it sounds like for a first-time owner still learning the bike

4. Budget beyond the sticker price

The purchase price is the smallest number in the first-year budget for most new owners.

  • Comprehensive insurance for a superbike, especially for a newer rider, is a real recurring cost — get a quote before you buy, not after
  • Proper gear (helmet, jacket, gloves, boots) done right runs into real money — don't budget zero for this
  • Service intervals on high-performance engines are often shorter and pricier than commuter bikes

5. Test ride with a checklist, not just a feeling

  • Can you flat-foot or at least confidently tip-toe the bike at a stop? Seat height matters more in daily riding than it seems in a showroom
  • Does the clutch and throttle feel manageable at parking-lot speeds, not just on the open road?
  • How does the bike feel at a genuine low-speed U-turn — this exposes weight and balance issues a straight-line test ride hides

Before you sign the paperwork

  • Insurance quote obtained and understood, not assumed
  • Full gear budgeted for, not treated as optional
  • Pre-purchase inspection done if buying used
  • Low-speed handling tested, not just straight-line feel
  • Honest answer to: will I actually ride this the way I'm imagining?

The right first superbike isn't the fastest one you can afford — it's the one that keeps you riding, learning, and upgrading your skill instead of your bike, for the first year or two.

Long-Distance Touring Prep: What Actually Matters Beyond a Full Tank

By Nandkishor Sharma · 06 September 2026 · 6 min read

A 300km+ day in the saddle exposes every small oversight you got away with on short rides. Sore wrists by hour two, a chain you meant to lube last week, a phone mount that vibrates loose — none of it matters on a 40-minute ride, all of it matters on a full-day tour.

1. The bike prep that actually gets skipped

Most riders check tyre pressure before a long ride. Fewer check the things that actually cause trouble hours in.

  • Chain tension and lube — a dry chain on a 400km day wears noticeably faster than the same distance split across a week
  • Fluid levels across the board — coolant, brake fluid, oil — not just the ones with a warning light
  • Fastener check on anything vibration can loosen — mirrors, luggage mounts, phone cradles
  • A spare visor or clear insert if you're leaving before sunrise and returning after dark

2. Ergonomics over distance change everything

What feels sporty for 40 minutes can genuinely hurt by hour three. Small adjustments made before you leave save real discomfort later.

  • Check lever reach and adjust if your bike allows it — small changes reduce hand fatigue significantly over hours
  • A tank bag or subtle seating adjustment can shift weight off your wrists on longer stretches
  • Plan a stop every 90 minutes to an hour on the first long tour — you'll learn your own limit and can stretch it out on future rides
Fatigue on a long ride doesn't announce itself — it just quietly slows your reactions until a small mistake becomes a real one.

3. Packing without turning the bike into a mule

  • Soft luggage (tail bags, tank bags) keeps weight low and centered better than overloaded backpacks, which fatigue your shoulders and back over distance
  • Distribute weight evenly — a lopsided load changes handling more than most riders expect
  • Pack a basic tool kit and a tyre repair kit — punctures on a touring route are rarely near a workshop

4. Route and fuel planning that isn't just "follow the GPS"

  • Mark fuel stops on the route in advance — some UAE highway stretches have longer gaps between stations than riders expect
  • Check your bike's real-world range at touring speed, not the optimistic spec-sheet number
  • Share your route and expected return time with someone, especially for routes with mountain or remote stretches

5. What to actually carry

  • More water than feels necessary — UAE heat dehydrates faster than most riders account for, even with good airflow gear
  • A basic first aid kit, small enough to not matter until the day it matters
  • Portable power bank for phone and GPS — a dead phone mid-route removes your safety net, not just your music

Before you roll out on a long tour

  • Chain, fluids, and fasteners checked — not just tyre pressure
  • Lever reach and riding position adjusted for comfort, not just sport
  • Fuel stops mapped against your bike's real-world range
  • Route and return time shared with someone
  • Water, basic tools, and a power bank packed

None of this is complicated, and none of it takes long to check. It's just the difference between a long ride that stays enjoyable at hour five, and one where you're counting down the kilometres.
